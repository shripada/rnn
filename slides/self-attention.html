Recurrent Neural Networks · Part 5

Every word looks at every word

Self-attention: drop the RNN, and let each word build its meaning by looking at the rest of the sentence.

Weights in the picture are illustrative, not from a trained model.

Part 4 ended with the Transformer table: keep the attention, drop the recurrence. Today we open that up. The picture is the whole talk: to work out what "it" means, the word "it" looks at every other word in its own sentence and pulls most of its meaning from "animal". No RNN carried that information along; "it" went and got it directly. The weights in the picture are illustrative.

Where we left off

The encoder still reads one step at a time

Part 4: the decoder looks back at every hj directly. But each hj was still built by an RNN reading the date one character after another. Read a sentence the same way, one word per step, and you get the chain on the left.

The question: if attention can link any two positions in one hop, why build the memories with a chain at all? Let the input words attend to each other.

This is point 2 from "What attention doesn't fix" in Part 4. Part 4's encoder read characters of a date; from here on the pieces are words of a sentence, the slide 1 sentence, but the chain is the same either way: one piece per step. Two costs of the chain: it's slow, because step t waits for step t − 1, so a GPU can't work along the sentence in parallel; and information between far-apart words still travels many steps, so it fades, as in Part 2. On the right, every word is wired to every other word, and all the wires can be computed at the same time.

The idea

Let the sentence attend to itself

Part 4 · attention

The decoder asks; the encoder's memories answer. Two different sequences.

Part 5 · self-attention

Every word asks, and every word of the same sentence answers. One sequence, looking at itself.

Each word xi gets a new vector zi: a mix of the whole sentence, weighted by what's useful to that word. The z's take the place of Part 4's encoder memories hj: they are what the decoder reads.

The goal, on one word. Translate “the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired” into French: “it” must become il if it means the animal (l'animal) or elle if it means the street (la rue). xit only says “a pronoun”, so a decoder reading it can't choose. Here “it” means the animal, so a good zit should carry “animal”.

The il/elle choice is the payoff to keep in mind: French nouns have gender, animal is masculine and street (la rue) feminine, so the translation of "it" depends on what "it" refers to. Swap "tired" for "wide" and the answer flips to elle; slide 34 comes back to that. Say the interview sentence: "self-attention is attention within one sequence: every position computes a weighted average over every position of the same sequence, including itself." On its own, the word "it" says almost nothing; its meaning is in another word. That makes it the cleanest test: if z_it ends up carrying "animal", the mixing worked. Every word gets its own z at the same time, but we follow just this one.

A first try

Mix the words, weighted by how well they match

For each word i, look at every word j, itself included. i is the word doing the looking (for us, “it”); j is each word being looked at. Four steps:

1
Score how well word j matches word i: the dot product of their vectors (slide 6 works one out).
eij = xi · xj
2
Make every score positive, with exp (slide 7 shows how).
exp(eij)
3
Divide by the total over every word k, so word i's weights add up to 1.
aij = exp(eij) / Σk exp(eik)
4
Mix: scale each word vector by its weight, and add them up over every word j.
zi = Σj aij xj

Steps 2 and 3 are Part 4's soften, in two steps.

Does this work? The next slides run it on three words, by hand.

This is Part 4's score, soften, mix, with nothing new: the word vectors x themselves do the matching and get mixed, so there are no weights to learn. Soften is split into its two halves, because the next slides do one step per slide. Σ_k means "add up over every word k"; k is a second name for "each word", needed because j is already used on top of the fraction. The first try, in numbers · setup

Three words, written as numbers

From “the animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired”, take three words. Each word's vector x is 3 numbers. Each number answers one question about the word: 1 = yes, 0 = no, 0.5 = sometimes. We picked the questions by hand so you can read the numbers; a trained model learns hundreds of numbers, with no labels.

The goal

A new vector zit for “it”, mixed from the words “it” relates to.

How: the four steps from the last slide (score, make positive, divide, mix), one slide each.

Read the table across: animal is a thing and alive; street is a thing but not alive; it is a pronoun, with a half for "alive" because "it" often refers to something alive. We follow one word, "it". In the formulas, i is the word doing the looking (here always "it") and j is the word being looked at, which runs over all three, including "it" itself.

The first try · step 1 of 4

Score: how well does each word match “it”?

The dot product of two vectors: pair up their numbers, multiply each pair, add the results. It gives one number, big when both vectors have big numbers in the same places. Call it the score eit,j: how well word j matches “it”.

The top score is “it” against itself. A vector dotted with itself is its squared length, 0² + 0.5² + 1², and another word can only beat that by being longer and pointing the same way. That's problem 1, in numbers.

Do the animal row aloud: 0 times 1, plus 0.5 times 1, plus 1 times 0, is 0.5. Each score is a single number, one per word j. The self-score is the largest because nothing matches a vector as well as itself: it has big numbers in exactly its own places. "Squared length" is the sum of its numbers squared (Pythagoras), which is what a vector dotted with itself is.

The first try · step 2 of 4

Make every score positive

A weight can't be negative, but a score can. So raise 2.718 to the power of each score. This is exp: exp(s) = 2.718s. (2.718… is the constant mathematicians call e. It is not the score eit,j; the two names clash.)

2.7180 = 1, so a score of 0 gives 1.

A negative score gives a number between 0 and 1. It never goes below 0.

Each +1 on a score multiplies its result by 2.718, so the top score pulls ahead.

Why 2.718 and not, say, 2 or 10? Any number above 1 would make scores positive and keep their order; a bigger base just makes the winner win harder. 2.718 is chosen because the slope of 2.718^s is 2.718^s itself, which makes the gradients in backprop simple. On a calculator: 2.718^0.5 is the square root of 2.718, about 1.65. Here no score is negative, but in a trained model plenty are: a dot product is negative when the two vectors point opposite ways.

The first try · step 3 of 4

Divide by the total, so the weights add up to 1

Steps 2 and 3 together are softmax, as in Part 4: ait,j = exp(eit,j) / Σk exp(eit,k). Σk means “add up over every word k”: it is the total above.

The weight a_it,j is the share of "it"'s attention that goes to word j. Dividing by the total is what makes the shares add up to 1 (100%), so the mix on the next slide is an average and not a sum that grows with the sentence. Point at the bars: "it" gives more than half of its attention to itself. The formula is the same softmax as Part 4; k is just a second name for "each word", needed because j is already used on the top of the fraction.

The first try · step 4 of 4

Mix: add up the vectors, weighted

Scale each word's vector xj by its weight, then add the results column by column. Vectors in, so a vector out.

zit = Σj ait,j xj (Σj: add up over every word j)
    = ait,animal xanimal + ait,street xstreet + ait,it xit

This is the step that confused people: the Σ adds vectors, not single numbers, so the answer is a vector. Each column is added on its own: the "is a thing" column is 0.27 + 0.16 + 0, and so on. The weights add up to 1, so z is a weighted average: each of its numbers lies between the smallest and biggest number in that column. How to judge the result: in this sentence "it" means the animal, so the best z_it could do is copy animal's vector. Read the two vectors column by column: wanted 1, 1, 0; got 0.43, 0.55, 0.57. The pronoun column, where animal has 0, came out the biggest.

The first try · what went wrong

Two problems, both from using x for everything

Problem 1 · it picks itself

Problem 2 · same both ways

The cause: xj says what word j is. xit should say what “it” is looking for, but xit = [0, 0.5, 1] only says what “it” is: a pronoun. So it finds the only pronoun around: itself.

The fix: give each word extra vectors, made from x by matrices that training can learn, so “what it is” and “what it looks for” can differ.

Problem 2 comes from the dot product itself: a·b = b·a, because each pair of numbers multiplies the same either way round. Any score built from one vector per word is symmetric. But the two directions are different questions: "it" is looking for a noun to take its meaning from (here: a thing that's alive); "animal" is looking for whatever helps "animal", which need not be "it". The deeper cause: the first try has no weights beyond the word vectors themselves, so training could only change x, and one x has to say both what a word is and what it is looking for. The fix starts on the next slide: learned matrices make extra vectors per word, and because the word asking and the word answering use different matrices, the score no longer has to be the same both ways.

The fix · make a query

Give “it” a query: what it's looking for

The query is a second vector for “it”, made from xit with a matrix WQ. x W (a vector times a matrix) means: scale each row of W by the matching number in x, then add the rows. WQ is hand-picked here; a trained model learns it.

The matrix

Its only non-zero row is the pronoun row: a pronoun goes looking for a thing that's alive.

3 rows, because x has 3 numbers. 3 columns by our choice, so q has 3 numbers and keeps x's labels. That length, called dk, must match the keys', so q and a key can be dotted.

qit = xit WQ, with xit = [0, 0.5, 1]

qit = [2, 2, 0] reads: looking for a thing (2) that is alive (2).

Do q_it aloud: x_it is [0, 0.5, 1], so take 0 of W_Q's first row, 0.5 of its second, and 1 of its third, and add: [2, 2, 0]. The query is not what "it" is (a pronoun); it is what "it" wants (a thing that's alive). That is the split the last slide asked for. The shape of W_Q: the rows are fixed by x (each number in x scales one row), the columns are a free choice that sets the length of q. We picked 3 so q reads with the same labels as x. A real model picks d_k as a setting; the original Transformer used x of 512 numbers and d_k = 64, with no human labels on the columns. Only "it" needs a query here, because we only follow "it"; in the real layer every word makes a query, a key and a value at the same time.

The fix · make the keys

Give every word a key: what a query is matched against

The query says what “it” is looking for. The key is the other side: a vector per word, describing what that word can be found by. The score will compare the query of “it” with each word's key. Each key is made from that word's x, the same way as the query, with another matrix WK.

So every key is x with its pronoun number set to 0. For “it”: xit = [0, 0.5, 1] → kit = [0, 0.5, 0]. “it” can no longer match itself by being a pronoun.

Read one row: animal's x is [1, 1, 0], so take 1 of W_K's thing row, 1 of its alive row and 0 of its pronoun row: [1, 1, 0]. The key is what a word puts on display for matching; the query is what a word goes looking for. Here the key is just x with the pronoun number removed, because nothing in this toy is looking for pronouns. A trained W_K is not this simple, but it does the same kind of job: it picks out the features worth being found by.

The fix · steps 1 to 3 again

Score the query against each key

The same three steps, but step 1 now scores the query of “it” against each word's key: eit,j = qit · kj, with qit = .

animal now gets most of the weight, and “it” gets little. No key has the pronoun number, so “it” no longer matches itself.

Walk across one row, animal: the score is 2·1 + 2·1 + 0·0 = 4, then 2.718^4 ≈ 54.6, then divide by the total. The weights still add up to 1 (up to rounding). Compare with step 3 of the first try: there "it" kept 57% for itself.

The fix · step 4 again

Now zit looks like “animal”

The query and key decide who gets picked. But step 4 still hands over each word's raw xj. Next: choose what gets handed over.

Same column-by-column add as before, with the new weights. Then the second problem. Compare weights, not raw scores: softmax turns each word's row of scores into weights on its own, so a score of 4 in one row and 0 in another can't be compared; only the shares within a row mean anything. "it" gives animal 84%. "animal" asking uses animal's query, which is all zeros (W_Q only reacts to the pronoun number), so every score in its row is 0 and its weights are even: 33% each, "it" included. 84% one way, 33% the other: two different matrices mean the two directions are no longer forced to be equal. An all-zero query is the toy's way of saying animal isn't looking for anything; a trained W_Q gives every word its own question. Both matrices are hand-picked here; in a model they are learned.

The fix · make the values

Give every word a value: what it hands over if picked

Start from the problem: the weights now pick well, but step 4 still hands over x, the very vector the key was made from. "it" keeps 4% of its own attention, so 4% of its own x comes back into z_it: its half-alive guess and its pronoun number, now looking like evidence about what "it" refers to. That's circular: "it" is using its own guess to confirm its own guess. So what gets handed over should be chosen separately from what gets matched, and that is the value. Why the −0.5? "it" is half alive in x only as a guess about what it refers to. That guess helps when matching, so the key keeps it. But it isn't something "it" knows to pass on, so the value cancels it: 0.5 of the alive row gives +0.5 alive, and 1 of the pronoun row gives −0.5 alive, which add to 0. animal and street have no pronoun number, so their values are just their thing and alive numbers. The point of this slide: the key and the value of the same word can differ, because one is for being found and the other for being handed over. A single vector could not do both.

The fix · step 4 with values

Mix the values: no more echo

Same weights as before, from the query and keys. Step 4 now scales and adds each word's value vj instead of its xj: zit = Σj ait,j vj.

Who to pick comes from q and k; what to hand over comes from v. Each has its own matrix, so each can be learned for its own job.

The weights are unchanged from slide 13: changing W_V does not change who is picked, only what arrives. Look at the "it" row of the table: with x it added [0, 0.02, 0.04] to its own z, an echo of its own guess and pronoun number; with values it adds [0, 0, 0]. Every number in z_it now comes from animal and street, the words "it" actually picked. The alive number drops from 0.86 to 0.84, and that's the point, not a loss: 0.02 of the old "alive" was "it" hearing its own guess back. In this toy the matching features and the handed-over features are almost the same; in real models they differ much more, which is why every word gets all three vectors.

Three jobs for every word

Query, key, value

The last slides gave each word three vectors, each made from its own x with a matrix that every word shares. In a real model these three matrices are learned: they start as random numbers and backprop adjusts them, like every weight since Part 1.

what it isin our example qi = xiWQquery: what I'm looking for kj = xjWKkey: what I offer, for matching vj = xjWVvalue: what I hand over if picked

A library: your query is what you type in the search box, the keys are the catalogue entries it's matched against, and the values are the books you take home.

This slide only names what the last slides built. The interview sentence: "self-attention is attention where the queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence: every position computes a weighted average over every position's values, including its own, with weights from its query against every key." Point at the last column: "it" can have a query that means "looking for a thing that's alive" while its key no longer says "I'm a pronoun", so it no longer matches itself best. The value is a separate choice: the features used to match aren't the ones you want handed over, and W_V lets the layer pick the second set (and, per head later, shrink it to d_v numbers). In the toy the matrices were hand-picked so the numbers read; in a model all three are learned from the loss alone.

The difference: in Part 4 the query came from one sequence (the decoder) and the keys from another (the encoder). Here all three come from the same sentence.

Part 4's score slide wrote the decoder side as W_s s_{t−1}, "what I need", and the encoder side as W_h h_j, "what's here"; its live-numbers slide labelled them q and k, and the values were the h_j themselves. Part 4 wrote matrix times column vector (W_s s); this deck writes row vector times matrix (x W_Q), the Transformer paper's convention. Same idea. One more difference: Part 4 combined query and key with a small network, v · tanh(q + k); self-attention uses a plain dot product, next slide. The swap point: the score is x_i W_Q · x_j W_K, which is x_i W_Q W_Kᵀ x_jᵀ; swapping i and j gives x_j W_Q W_Kᵀ x_iᵀ, a different number unless W_Q W_Kᵀ is symmetric. Why two matrices W_Q, W_K and not one d × d matrix M = W_Q W_Kᵀ? The score is the same either way, but the two matrices hold 2·d·d_k numbers against d² for M, fewer only when d_k is less than d/2: in the original Transformer, 2·512·64 = 65,536 against 512² = 262,144. And the split is what lets each head score in its own small space of d_k numbers. Shapes, for the whiteboard: x_i has d numbers (512 in the original Transformer); W_Q and W_K are d × d_k, W_V is d × d_v.

Try it · the √dk

Why divide by √dk?

One query, six keys, every number drawn at random with average 0 and spread 1, as at the start of training. Change dk:

dk (length of q and k) =
softmax(q · k)
softmax(q · k / √dk)

A dot product adds up dk products, each of typical size 1, so its typical size (root-mean-square) is √dk. Unscaled, one weight hits ~100%: softmax is saturated, and blame barely flows back.

Try d_k = 4 first: both sides look alike. Then 64, the size each head uses in the original Transformer: the left side puts nearly everything on one key, while the right side still spreads. At 1024 the left side is a hard pick. Why it matters for training: when softmax is that sharp, nudging any score barely changes the weights, so the gradients are tiny. That's the vanishing blame from Part 2 again, in a new place. Where the expected size comes from: each product q_c·k_c of two independent spread-1 numbers has average 0 and variance 1; a score adds d_k of them, so its variance is d_k and its typical size is √d_k (8 at d_k = 64). The caption's number is measured from only 6 scores, so it wanders around that, often by 30%; click "New random vectors" to show it. Dividing by √d_k brings the spread of the scores back to about 1, whatever d_k is. The vectors here are random, as at the start of training.

The real recipe · ÷√dk · step 1

Divide the scores by √3

Back to “it”, with the full recipe. Slide 13 skipped one thing: qit and each key have 3 numbers, so dk = 3, and every score is divided by .

Why: a dot product adds up dk products, so it grows with dk. Dividing by √dk keeps scores near size 1, so softmax doesn't snap to one word (the dk demo showed this).

Same q_it and keys as slide 13, same scores 4, 2 and 1; only the division is new. With d_k = 3 the division is small; with d_k = 64, as in the original Transformer, it is a division by 8, and that's where it matters. Do one row aloud: 4 / 1.73 = 2.31.

The real recipe · ÷√dk · steps 2 and 3

Make positive and divide, again

With hand-picked matrices this makes “it” pick less sharply. A trained model learns its matrices with the division in place, so it learns bigger scores where it needs a sharp pick.

Same two steps as slides 7 and 8: exp each scaled score, add them up, divide by the total. Compare with the last column, slide 13's weights without the division: smaller scores mean smaller gaps between them, and exp turns smaller gaps into more even weights. animal still wins clearly. Our matrices were chosen before we divided by √3, so here the division just softens; a trained W_Q and W_K would have been learned with it and would come out bigger to compensate where a sharp choice helps.

All words at once

The whole sentence is a few matrix multiplies

Stack our three words' x vectors as the rows of one matrix X. Then every word's q, k, v, scores, weights and z come out together, one row per word. The it row of each is the one we worked by hand.

Z = softmax( Q K⊤ / √dk ) V with Q = XWQ, K = XWK, V = XWV

K⊤ is K with its rows turned into columns, so row i, column j of Q K⊤ is qi · kj. Sizes are rows × columns: T = number of words (3 here), d = numbers per x (3), dk = numbers per query and key, dv = per value (3 each, our choice).

No row waits for another. A GPU does them all in one go: that's the speed-up over the RNN.

Point at the "it" rows: q_it = [2, 2, 0] from slide 11, its scores 2.31, 1.15, 0.58 from slide 21, its weights from slide 22, z_it from slide 23. The other rows are the same recipe for animal and street, done at the same time. In this toy only "it" has a query (W_Q's only non-zero row is the pronoun row), so animal's and street's scores are all 0 and their weights are an even third each; a trained W_Q gives every word a query. This is the equation from "Attention Is All You Need", worth writing from memory. Softmax is applied row by row. The T × T matrix is why long inputs are expensive: double the length, four times the scores. That's the same cost as Part 4's T × T′ map, now inside the encoder too. Ask: "how many learned weights does one self-attention layer have, and does it depend on T?" Three matrices, d × d_k each; no, it doesn't depend on T. That's why it handles any length, like the parameter sharing in Part 1.

All words at once · in code

The same equation is four lines of code

In PyTorch, @ is matrix multiply and K.T is K⊤. Each line makes one or two of the last slide's matrices, and does one or two of the four steps:

Q, K, V = X @ W_Q, X @ W_K, X @ W_V      # step 0: (T, d_k), (T, d_k), (T, d_v)
scores  = Q @ K.T / math.sqrt(d_k)       # step 1, score: (T, T)
weights = scores.softmax(-1)             # steps 2–3, make positive, divide: (T, T)
Z       = weights @ V                    # step 4, mix: (T, d_v)

softmax(-1) works along each row, so each word's weights add up to 1. Run on our toy's X and the matrices from slides 11–15, the “it” row of Z is the zit from slide 23.

These lines, with heads and a batch of sentences added, are MultiHeadAttention.forward in Part 6's demo/transformer.py.

This closes the loop from the equation to what you will actually write. Read each line against the last slide's matrices: X is T × d, so X @ W_Q is T × d_k; Q @ K.T is T × T, one row per word asking, one column per word answered; softmax(-1) means along the last dimension, the row; weights @ V is T × d_v, one new vector per word. Nothing loops over words: that's the "no step waits" from the last slide, in code. In demo/transformer.py the same four lines appear with two changes: Q, K and V are split into heads (view and transpose give shape n × heads × T × d_k, n sentences at once), and a masked_fill line sits between scores and softmax; the masking slide explains it.

The catch

Shuffle the words, get the same answers

“The animal didn't cross the street” and “the street didn't cross the animal” mean different things. Run a tiny self-attention layer on their three content words. Each word starts as 8 random numbers (its x), and WQ, WK, WV are random too: nothing is trained. Each row is that word's output z, rounded. Compare animal on the left with animal on the right.

Why random numbers: our 3-number toy has no vector for “cross”, and the point holds for any vectors. Cell colour is only a guide: rust positive, blue negative.

Nothing in score, soften or mix knows where a word is. Without positions, self-attention can't tell order: shuffle the words, and the outputs are shuffled the same way, nothing more.

This is the price of dropping the RNN. An RNN knows order for free: it reads in order. Self-attention treats the input as a set: the score between "animal" and "street" is the same wherever each one sits, so every word's output is the same, just listed in a different order. The technical name is "permutation equivariant": shuffle the input, and the output is shuffled the same way, nothing more. The strips are real computations, done in the browser with random weights; the point holds for any weights and any word vectors, which is why we can leave the hand-made toy here.

The fix

Add a position to each word

Before the first layer, add a position vector to each word vector: xi + pi, where i is the word's position, counting from 0. Now “animal” at position 0 and “animal” at position 2 are different inputs.

The original Transformer used fixed waves: each column is a sine or cosine of the position, slower and slower to the right. Many later models (BERT, GPT) learn pi instead.

pi: one row per position, 0 at the top to 23
Drawn with 32 columns so the waves show; the demo next uses 8.
The formula, if asked: p(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d)), p(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d)). The left columns change fast, like the seconds hand of a clock; the right columns change slowly, like the hour hand. Together they give every position a unique pattern. The paper chose waves partly because a fixed offset (k positions later) is a simple rotation of the pattern, which may make relative positions easy to learn. Newer models often use other schemes that encode relative distance directly; that's beyond this course.

The fix · in numbers

Run it again, with positions

The same layer and the same random word vectors. Before scoring, the word at position i (0, 1 or 2) gets pi added: 8 numbers, two per wave. Each wave first divides i by a number, 1, 10, 100 or 1000, then takes the sine and the cosine of the result. Dividing by more gives a slower wave, so the right-hand columns barely change from one position to the next.

For example, p1's first number is sin(1) = 0.84, and its third is sin(1 ÷ 10) = sin(0.1) = 0.10. Angles are in radians.

Read the table: position 0 is all sines of 0 and cosines of 0, so 0 and 1 alternating; positions 1 and 2 differ mostly in the left columns, the fast waves. The divisors 1, 10, 100, 1000 are 10000^(2k/d) with d = 8 and k = 0..3, the formula from slide 27's notes. "animal" gets p_0 in the first sentence and p_2 in the second, so its input differs, and so does its output. Toggle the button to compare with no positions: back to identical. With positions, the model can tell who crossed whom.

One head isn't enough

One head gives one blended answer

Everything so far, one WQ, WK, WV and one row of weights per word, is called one attention head.

In “the animal didn't cross the street”, the word cross wants two things: who crossed (animal) and what was crossed (street). Their values, from slide 15:

“cross” has no toy vector, but it doesn't need one here: only the values it mixes matter. The 50/50 split is hand-picked.

The real limit: one head has one query, so one set of weights per word, so one mixture. When a word needs two different words, it gets back an average of both.

A word often needs several kinds of context at once: its subject, its object, the word next to it, the noun a pronoun points to. A verb like "cross" wants both who did it and what it was done to. One head has one query per word, so one row of weights; spread it over two words and step 4 averages their values. Here half animal plus half street is [1, 0.5, 0]: "a thing, sometimes alive", which is neither the animal nor the street. Two separate heads, each with its own query, can each put its weight on one word, and gluing their outputs end to end keeps both: who, then what, 6 numbers. The values are the toy's own from slide 15.

The heads don't share weights, so each learns its own idea of what to look for. Because each head works with d/H numbers, eight heads of 64 cost about the same as one head of 512: you get eight different ways of looking for the same price. W_O is d × d; it lets the next layer use any combination of what the heads found. [a ; b] means glue end to end, as in Part 4. Nobody assigns jobs to heads: like Part 4's attention, they're learned from the loss alone. Also, d_k = 64 is the d_k from the √d_k slide.

Three patterns that people have found in trained Transformers: heads that look at the previous word, heads that link a word to the one it depends on grammatically (a verb to its subject), and heads that link a pronoun to the noun it refers to. A good paper on this is Clark et al. (2019), "What Does BERT Look At?". Try "cross" in each head, then "it" in head 2. Caveat: real heads are messier than this, and many don't have a clean job.

Writing, not just reading

No peeking at the next word

A model that writes, one word at a time, learns by guessing each next word, all positions at once.

If “didn't” could look ahead at “cross”, guessing “cross” would be trivial. So before softmax, every score for a later word is set to −∞: its weight becomes exactly 0.

This is masked self-attention: the decoder of the original Transformer, and the whole of GPT. In slide 25's code it is one more line, before softmax:

scores = scores.masked_fill(~allowed, float("-inf"))

Weights in the map are illustrative.

This is teacher forcing from Part 3, done in parallel. The RNN decoder never saw the future because it hadn't computed it yet. A Transformer sees the whole target sentence at once during training, so the mask is what keeps it honest. When generating, it still writes one word at a time, feeding each word back in, as in Part 3. An encoder like BERT's has no mask: every word sees both sides, which is what "bidirectional" means in its title. In the code line, allowed is a T × T table of True/False, True where looking is allowed (on and below the diagonal); ~ flips it, so masked_fill writes −∞ into every later word's score. demo/transformer.py does exactly this. Weights shown are illustrative.

Stacking · back to zit

Layer 2 needs a vector for “it”: which one?

One layer (the four steps, for every word) lets each word gather from the others once. Models stack layers: layer 2 takes layer 1's outputs as its word vectors, with its own WQ, WK, WV, so “it” can ask a better question using what it gathered. The original Transformer stacks 6. So what should the vector of “it” be for layer 2? Back to the zit we kept, which came :

replace: “it” becomes zit
add back: “it” becomes xit + zit

Adding is the skip connection: x skips around the attention and is added to what comes out. “it” keeps what it was; z only adds context. To add them, z must be as long as x: here dv = d = 3; with several heads, WO brings the glued heads back to length d (slide 30).

This picks up the z_it we kept from the √d_k rerun, now that there's somewhere for it to go. Stacking is the reason: one layer gathers context once; in layer 2, "it" asks its question with a vector that already carries what it gathered, so it can ask a better question, and each layer gathers more. That means each layer must hand the next one vector per word. Replacing x with z is the obvious first try, and the numbers show why it fails: z_it is mostly animal's value, so the new "it" is nearly animal's vector, and its pronoun number is 0: nothing in it says this word was ever a pronoun. After a few layers of replacing, every word would be a blend of blends. Adding keeps x_it whole and puts the context on top: still a pronoun, now also a thing and alive. Also called a residual connection. It's why d_v has to equal d: you can only add vectors of the same length; with several heads, W_O brings the glued heads back to length d (slide 30). Next: what stacking buys. The tired/wide slide uses exactly this x_it + z_it in layer 2. After that, the block diagram draws the skip as an arrow around the attention box joining its output at a +, and gives a second reason for it: the + is a short path for blame, like the LSTM's cell state in Part 2.

Why stack

Why stack: what does “it” refer to?

layerlast word

Weights are illustrative, not from a trained model. Trained models do show patterns like this.

A well-known example from Google's 2017 blog post introducing the Transformer: change one word at the far end and "it" should switch from "animal" to "street", because animals get tired and streets are wide. Start in layer 1 and toggle the last word: the boxes for animal and street don't move. They can't: in layer 1 the weights from "it" to animal and to street come from q_it, k_animal and k_street, and none of those is made from the last word. What layer 1 can do is gather the last word itself into z_it. Now switch to layer 2: the vector of "it" there is x_it + z_it, which carries "tired" or "wide", so its query can look for something that gets tired or something that is wide. Toggle the last word again and the weight moves. The "it" position can reach any word in one hop, but using what one word says to choose among the others takes two layers. That's the reason to stack. Back to slide 3's goal: in layer 2, "it" ends up carrying animal or street, so a French decoder can pick il or elle; after layer 1 alone, it couldn't. Compare an RNN reading left to right: it has already processed "it" by the time it reads "tired", so in a one-direction RNN the memory at "it" can't use that word at all.

Putting it together

One Transformer block

  • Each sublayer has a skip connection around it: x + sublayer(x), as on the stacking slide.
  • Then a norm (layer normalisation): shift and scale each word's numbers so they average 0 with spread 1.
  • The skips are a short path for blame, like Part 2's cell state; with the norms, they're a big part of why deep stacks train at all: 6 blocks in the original encoder, 24 in BERT-large, 96 in GPT-3.
  • After the last block, each word's vector feeds the prediction: the next word (GPT) or a hidden word (BERT, Part 7).
Norm keeps the numbers at a steady size from block to block, so no block's output blows up or shrinks away. The original paper puts the norm after the add, as drawn; most newer models put it before the sublayer instead, which trains more stably. The block counts: 6 encoder blocks in "Attention Is All You Need", 24 in BERT-large (Devlin et al., 2018), 96 in GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020). Stacking is the key idea: in block 2, "it" asks its question with a vector that already carries what it gathered in block 1, so it can ask a better question, and each block gathers more context. Only at the very end does a word's vector get turned into a prediction. A translation Transformer also has Part 4's cross-attention: decoder queries, encoder keys and values; Part 6 opens this up and builds the full encoder–decoder. Ask: "which of the four earlier parts shows up here?" The skip path echoes Part 2's protected path; cross-attention is Part 4; the encoder–decoder shape is Part 3; and the idea of one set of weights used at every position is Part 1's parameter sharing.

Inside the block

The feed-forward network: each word alone

So after attention, each word goes through a small network of its own, the feed-forward network:

FFN(a) = max(0, a W1 + b1) W2 + b2
  • a: one word's vector after attention. W1, W2: learned matrices; b1, b2: learned bias vectors.
  • Sizes: d → 4d → d, 512 → 2048 → 512 in the original.
  • max(0, ·) zeroes negatives: max(0, [0.5, −0.3, 1.2]) = [0.5, 0, 1.2].
  • One word at a time: moving information between words stays attention's job.
The next slide draws it, with our numbers. Why it's needed: without it, each word's output is just a weighted average of value vectors. The softmax picks the weights, but the values themselves only get scaled and added, so features are never combined in a non-linear way: attention can't compute "a thing AND alive" as its own feature. Point at z_it: each of its numbers lies between the smallest and largest value in that column, which is what an average does. The FFN is the same kind of layer as in any plain neural network: multiply by a matrix, add a bias, zero out the negatives (ReLU), multiply by another matrix, add a bias. Zeroing the negatives is the non-linear step: that bend is what lets it combine features. The same W_1, W_2 are used for every word, Part 1's parameter sharing again.

Inside the block · the FFN, drawn

One unit that fires for “a thing and alive”

Left: the 3 numbers of one word's vector a. Middle: the 12 numbers of max(0, a W1 + b1). We hand-pick one middle unit (weights +1, +1, 0, bias −1.5); a trained FFN learns them all.

input

Every word goes through this same network on its own. (The real block normalises a first; skipped to keep the numbers readable.)

Click through the four inputs in order. "it" before attention: alive is only 0.5 and thing is 0, so the sum is negative and the unit gives 0. After attention and the skip, x_it + z_it carries "a thing" and "alive" from the animal, and the unit fires. animal itself fires too. street is a thing but not alive: the sum is −0.5, and max(0, ·) turns it to 0. That zero is the bend: without max, the unit would be a straight line, and adding up straight lines only ever gives another straight line, which is what attention's averages already are. With the bend, the unit answers a question no single input number answers: is this both a thing and alive? The middle layer has 4d units, 12 for our d = 3, 2048 in the original; W_2 then mixes them back down to d numbers, which the skip connection adds to the word. The weights and the bias here are hand-picked; the other 11 units and W_2 are left unlabelled because we haven't picked numbers for them.

The trade

RNN layer vs self-attention layer

RNNself-attention
steps that must wait for each otherT1
hops between two far-apart wordsup to T1
work: each word through d × d matricesT · d²T · d²
work: every word scored and mixed with every wordnoneT² · d
knows word orderfor freeonly if you add positions
memory while generatingfixed: one stategrows with every word written

T = words in the sentence, d = length of each vector. Rows 1, 2 and 4 are Table 1 of “Attention Is All You Need”; row 3 is the cost of WQ, WK, WV, WO, which that table leaves out. Work counts operations, not seconds.

Self-attention never does less work than the RNN: it does the same T · d², plus T² · d. What it wins is the first two rows: no step waits for another, and any two words are one hop apart.

Rows 1, 2 and 4 are Table 1 of "Attention Is All You Need". That table's "per layer" cost for self-attention is only row 4, the scoring and mixing; the RNN's T · d² is its d × d matrix applied at every step. A real self-attention layer also multiplies every word by W_Q, W_K, W_V and W_O, four d × d matrices: that's row 3, the same order of work as the RNN. So the extra T² · d is small when T is below d (with d = 512, a 50-word sentence) and dominates when T is far above it (a book of 100,000 words), which is why long-context Transformers are an active research area. Work counts operations, not seconds: real speed depends on the hardware, and an RNN's T · d² comes as T steps in a row while self-attention's comes all at once, which a GPU does far faster. The last row is where RNNs still win: an RNN generating text carries one fixed-size state, while a Transformer has to keep keys and values for every earlier word. That's why RNN-like and "state space" models are being revived for long streams.

A little history

From a side idea to the whole model

2016Jianpeng Cheng, Li Dong and Mirella Lapata, Long Short-Term Memory-Networks for Machine Reading: an LSTM that attends to its own earlier words (“intra-attention”). Ankur Parikh and colleagues, A Decomposable Attention Model for Natural Language Inference: attention with no RNN at all.
2017Zhouhan Lin and colleagues, A Structured Self-Attentive Sentence Embedding: several attention “hops” over one sentence.
2017Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, Attention Is All You Need: scaled dot-product self-attention, multiple heads, position encodings. The Transformer.
2018Jacob Devlin and colleagues, BERT: a Transformer encoder, pre-trained on lots of text. Alec Radford and colleagues (OpenAI), the first GPT: a Transformer decoder.

Orange: authors · blue: paper titles (click to open on arXiv)

Self-attention existed before 2017, mostly bolted onto an RNN. The Transformer paper's claim was in the title: you don't need the RNN at all. Its full title is worth knowing for an interview. BERT's full title is "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding". The first GPT paper is "Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training" (2018), published by OpenAI, not on arXiv. BERT uses the encoder half, GPT the decoder half; the masking slide showed the difference.

Be ready to answer

Questions

  1. What is self-attention? (every position scores every position of the same sequence, softmax, weighted sum of values)
  2. What are queries, keys and values? (what I'm looking for, what I offer for matching, what I hand over; three learned projections of each word)
  3. Why divide the scores by √dk? (dot products grow with dk; softmax saturates and gradients vanish)
  4. Why does a Transformer need position information? (self-attention is order-blind: shuffle the words, the outputs just shuffle)
  5. Why several heads? (one head returns one weighted average; several heads keep separate mixtures, each in its own learned space, at the same cost)
  6. What's the shape of the attention weights for T words? (T × T)
  7. Why mask the decoder? (so a word can't see the words it's being trained to predict)
  8. When does an RNN still make sense? (long streams with fixed memory, one step at a time)
Cover the hints, ask each question cold, and have someone answer out loud in one or two sentences. Question 1 should come with the equation: softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V.

Recap

Remember:

  1. The goal: a new vector per word that carries its meaning in this sentence, built without an RNN. zit carries “animal”, so a decoder can translate “it” as il.
  2. Self-attention: every word makes a query, key and value; scores are q · k / √dk; softmax; mix the values.
  3. It's order-blind, so add a position to each word.
  4. Multi-head: several small attentions in parallel, glued and mixed. Stack blocks with skip connections: that's the Transformer.
Ask someone to write Z = softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V from memory and give the shape of every matrix for T words.
