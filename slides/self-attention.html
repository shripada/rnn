Recurrent Neural Networks · Part 5

Every word looks at every word

Self-attention: drop the RNN, and let each word build its meaning by looking at the rest of the sentence.

Weights in the picture are illustrative, not from a trained model.

Part 4 ended with the Transformer table: keep the attention, drop the recurrence. Today we open that up. The picture is the whole talk: to work out what "it" means, the word "it" looks at every other word in its own sentence and pulls most of its meaning from "animal". No RNN carried that information along; "it" went and got it directly. The weights in the picture are illustrative.

Where we left off

The encoder still reads one step at a time

Part 4: the decoder looks back at every hj directly. But each hj was still built by an RNN, one character after another.

The question: if attention can link any two positions in one hop, why build the memories with a chain at all? Let the input words attend to each other.

This is point 2 from "What attention doesn't fix" in Part 4. Two costs of the chain: it's slow, because step t waits for step t − 1, so a GPU can't work along the sentence in parallel; and information between far-apart words still travels many steps, so it fades, as in Part 2. On the right, every word is wired to every other word, and all the wires can be computed at the same time.

The idea

Let the sentence attend to itself

Part 4 · attention

The decoder asks; the encoder's memories answer. Two different sequences.

Part 5 · self-attention

Every word asks, and every word of the same sentence answers. One sequence, looking at itself.

  • Each word xi gets a new vector zi: a mix of the whole sentence, weighted by what's useful to that word.
  • A first try, with Part 4's three verbs, for word i looking at every word j (itself included). Score: eij = xi · xj. Soften word i's scores into weights that add up to 1: aij = exp(eij) / Σk exp(eik). Mix the xj themselves: zi = Σj aij xj.
Problem 1: a word is usually most similar to itself. “it” would look at “it”, not at “animal”.
Problem 2: the score is symmetric, the same both ways: xit·xanimal = xanimal·xit. But “it” is looking for a noun; “animal” isn't looking for a pronoun.
Say the interview sentence: "self-attention is attention where the queries, keys and values all come from the same sequence; every position computes a weighted average over every position, including itself." The first try is deliberately naive, and it fails for a reason worth understanding: x_i · x_i is the squared length of x_i, usually the biggest score in its row. And it forces one vector to do two jobs: say what this word is, and say what it's looking for. A pronoun is looking for a noun, which is not what a pronoun is. The second problem comes from the same place: a dot product doesn't care about order, so x_it · x_animal = x_animal · x_it. Any score built from one vector per word is symmetric, but "who is looking for whom" isn't: "it" needs "animal", "animal" doesn't need "it". The next slide works both out in numbers; the one after splits those jobs.

The first try, in numbers

Why “it” ends up looking at itself

Three words from the sentence. Each x is 3 hand-picked numbers meaning is a thing, is alive, is a pronoun, so you can read them; a trained model learns its own. Follow the word it, so i = it, and j runs over all three words:

score with

Start on "first try" and do one score aloud: x_it · x_animal = 0·1 + 0.5·1 + 1·0 = 0.5. Then the self-score: x_it · x_it = 0.25 + 1 = 1.25, which is just x_it's squared length. A dot product of a vector with itself is its squared length, and no other vector can beat that unless it is longer and points nearly the same way. So softmax gives "it" most of the weight, and the mix z_it is still mostly "pronoun". Then click "a separate query and key". The query q_it = x_it W_Q is a different vector from x_it: W_Q turns "I am a pronoun" into "looking for a thing that is alive". The key k = x W_K keeps "thing" and "alive" and drops "pronoun", so "it" no longer matches its own key well. Now "animal" wins and z_it looks like "animal". Then point at the "symmetric" / "one-way" line: in the first try x_animal · x_it is the same 0.5 as x_it · x_animal; with a query and key, "animal" asking about "it" scores q_animal · k_it = 0 while "it" asking about "animal" scores 4. Two different matrices make the score one-way. Last, point out that z is still a mix of the raw x_j: what gets handed over hasn't changed yet. Both matrices are hand-picked here; in a model they are learned. The next slide names these, and adds a third one, value, for what gets handed over. The real recipe also divides scores by √d_k; that comes two slides later.

Three jobs for every word

Query, key, value

Each word makes three vectors from its own xi, with three matrices shared by every word. They are learned: they start as random numbers and backprop adjusts them, like every weight since Part 1.

what it isin Part 4 qi = xiWQquery: what I'm looking forthe decoder's st−1 (through Ws) kj = xjWKkey: what I offer, for matchingeach hj (through Wh) vj = xjWVvalue: what I hand over if pickedeach hj itself

The query and key fix both problems from the first try. Why a third vector? The key only gets word j picked (a big weight aij). The value is what word j passes on once picked, so zi mixes the vj, not the xj.

A library: your query is what you type in the search box, the keys are the catalogue entries it's matched against, and the values are the books you take home.

You've already met all three roles, without the names: Part 4's slide on the score said the decoder side is a "query", the encoder side a "key", and what gets mixed are the "values". Self-attention just makes all three from the same word with three different learned matrices. That fixes the first try: "it" can have a query that means "looking for a noun that could be tired" while its key says "I'm a pronoun", so it no longer matches itself best. And the score is now x_i W_Q W_Kᵀ x_jᵀ: because W_Q ≠ W_K, swapping i and j changes it, so it can be one-way. The value is a separate choice: the features used to match aren't the ones you want handed over, and W_V lets the layer pick the second set (and, per head later, shrink it to d_v). Why two matrices W_Q, W_K and not one d × d matrix M = W_Q W_Kᵀ? Two thin d × d_k matrices have fewer weights, and are what lets each head score in its own small space. Shapes, for the whiteboard: x_i has d numbers (512 in the original Transformer); W_Q and W_K are d × d_k, W_V is d × d_v. Row vectors times matrices is the paper's convention.

How it works · one word

Score with a dot product, then scale it

1
Make a query for word i, and a key and value for every word j.
qi = xiWQquery: word i's x times WQ
kj = xjWKkey of every word j
vj = xjWVvalue of every word j
2
Score: dot product of query and key, divided by √dk, where dk is the length of each query and key.
eij = qi · kj / √dk
3
Soften word i's scores against every word j into weights.
aij = exp(eij) / ( exp(ei1) + … + exp(eiT) )
softmax: word i's weights add up to 1
4
Mix the values.
zi = ai1v1 + ai2v2 + … + aiTvT

Part 4 scored with a tiny network (Bahdanau's additive score). The dot product is Luong's: no extra weights, and it turns into one matrix multiply for the whole sentence.

Step 1 is three separate lines: each word's vector x times three different learned matrices (x W means vector times matrix, no symbol). The only dot product on this slide is in step 2: q_i · k_j. Same score–soften–mix. Two changes from Part 4. The score is a dot product, big when query and key point the same way. And it's divided by √d_k, where d_k is how many numbers are in each query and key (64 in the original Transformer). The paper calls this "scaled dot-product attention". The next slide shows why the division is there. Every word does this at the same time, and none of them waits for any other.

Try it · the √dk

Why divide by √dk?

One query, six keys, every number drawn at random with average 0 and spread 1, as at the start of training. Change dk:

dk (length of q and k) =
softmax(q · k)
softmax(q · k / √dk)

A dot product adds up dk products, each of typical size 1, so its typical size is √dk. Unscaled, one weight hits ~100%: softmax is saturated, and blame barely flows back.

Try d_k = 4 first: both sides look alike. Then 64, the size each head uses in the original Transformer: the left side puts nearly everything on one key, while the right side still spreads. At 1024 the left side is a hard pick. Why it matters for training: when softmax is that sharp, nudging any score barely changes the weights, so the gradients are tiny. That's the vanishing blame from Part 2 again, in a new place. Where the expected size comes from: each product q_c·k_c of two independent spread-1 numbers has average 0 and variance 1; a score adds d_k of them, so its variance is d_k and its typical size is √d_k (8 at d_k = 64). The caption's number is measured from only 6 scores, so it wanders around that, often by 30%; click "New random vectors" to show it. Dividing by √d_k brings the spread of the scores back to about 1, whatever d_k is. The vectors here are random, as at the start of training.

One row, by hand

Three words, d = dk = dv = 2. The word vectors x and the matrices WQ, WK, WV are hand-picked small numbers so the arithmetic is easy; a trained model learns them. Follow the word sat asking the sentence:

The other two rows work the same way, with their own query. All three can be done at the same time. zsat is what sat gathered from the sentence; the next slide shows where it goes.

Good to do at the whiteboard: copy x and the three matrices, then ask someone to compute q for "sat", the three scores, and the weights, before revealing them. Key point to check: the query is sat's, but the keys and values are everyone's, including sat's own. The output z_sat is a weighted average of the three value vectors, so it lands between them, closest to the value with the biggest weight.

Worked example, continued

What zsat is for

xsat says what “sat” means in any sentence; zsat is what it gathered from this one (). The rest of the model works on the two combined:

1
Add back. Keep what sat was, add what it gathered: the skip connection. Needs dv = d (here 2).
2
Tidy, then think. norm (layer normalisation) rescales to a steady size; FFN (feed-forward network, two layers) works on sat alone.
a = norm( xsat + zsat )
x′sat = norm( a + FFN(a) )
3
Next block. x′sat is sat's input there, with new WQ, WK, WV: it asks with a vector that already holds some of “cat”.
q′sat = x′satW′Q, k′sat = x′satW′K, …
4
The task. After the last block (6 in the original), each word's vector feeds the prediction: next word (GPT) or a hidden word (BERT, Part 7).
xsat → block 1 → block 2 → … → block N → guess

Only step 1 has numbers: this toy has no norm or FFN weights. Steps 1 and 2 together are one Transformer block, drawn on slide 19.

This answers "what do we do with z?". z is not an answer on its own; it is a context update for one word. Step 1: the skip connection adds it to the word's own vector instead of replacing it, so the word keeps its own meaning and z only adds context. That is also why d_v has to equal d; with several heads, W_O brings the glued heads back to length d (slide 16). Step 2: norm and FFN are only named here; slide 19 draws the whole block and Part 6 opens it up. Step 3 is the key idea of stacking: in block 2, "sat" asks its question with a vector that already knows about "cat", so it can ask a better question, and each block gathers more context. Step 4: only at the very end does a word's vector get turned into a prediction.

What it's for

What does “it” refer to?

last word

Weights are illustrative, not from a trained model. Trained models do show patterns like this.

A well-known example from Google's 2017 blog post introducing the Transformer. Change one word at the far end of the sentence and "it" should switch from "animal" to "street": animals get tired, streets are wide. An RNN reading left to right has already processed "it" by the time it reads "tired"; in a one-direction RNN, the memory at "it" can't use that. In self-attention the "it" position sees the whole sentence, the words after it included, in one hop. The weights shown are illustrative, not from a trained model. Trained Transformers do have heads that behave much like this.

All words at once

The whole sentence is a few matrix multiplies

Stack the T word vectors as rows of X. Three multiplies make every word's Q, K and V; two more (Q K⊤, then the weights times V) do every row's score and mix together:

Z = softmax( Q K⊤ / √dk ) V with Q = XWQ, K = XWK, V = XWV
thingshapewhat it holds
XT × dT words, one per row, each d numbers long
Q, KT × dka query and a key per word, dk numbers each (same length, to take q · k)
Q K⊤T × Tevery q · k at once (K⊤: K's rows as columns): the attention map before softmax
V, ZT × dva value per word, dv numbers each; a new vector z per word

No step waits for another. A GPU does this in one go: that's the speed-up over the RNN.

This is the equation from "Attention Is All You Need", worth writing from memory. Softmax is applied row by row. The T × T matrix is why long inputs are expensive: double the length, four times the scores. That's the same cost as Part 4's T × T′ map, now inside the encoder too. Ask: "how many learned weights does one self-attention layer have, and does it depend on T?" Three matrices, d × d_k each; no, it doesn't depend on T. That's why it handles any length, like the parameter sharing in Part 1.

A little history

From a side idea to the whole model

2016Jianpeng Cheng, Li Dong and Mirella Lapata, Long Short-Term Memory-Networks for Machine Reading: an LSTM that attends to its own earlier words (“intra-attention”). Ankur Parikh and colleagues, A Decomposable Attention Model for Natural Language Inference: attention with no RNN at all.
2017Zhouhan Lin and colleagues, A Structured Self-Attentive Sentence Embedding: several attention “hops” over one sentence.
2017Ashish Vaswani and colleagues, Attention Is All You Need: scaled dot-product self-attention, multiple heads, position encodings. The Transformer.
2018Jacob Devlin and colleagues, BERT: a Transformer encoder, pre-trained on lots of text. Alec Radford and colleagues (OpenAI), the first GPT: a Transformer decoder.

Orange: authors · blue: paper titles (click to open on arXiv)

Self-attention existed before 2017, mostly bolted onto an RNN. The Transformer paper's claim was in the title: you don't need the RNN at all. Its full title is worth knowing for an interview. BERT's full title is "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding". The first GPT paper is "Improving Language Understanding by Generative Pre-Training" (2018), published by OpenAI, not on arXiv. BERT uses the encoder half, GPT the decoder half; we'll see the difference on the masking slide.

The catch

Shuffle the words, get the same answers

A tiny self-attention layer, run on two sentences. Each word starts as 8 random numbers (its vector x), and WQ, WK, WV are random too: nothing is trained. Each row is that word's output z, its 8 numbers rounded to 2 decimals. Compare dog on the left with dog on the right.

Cell colour is only a guide: rust positive, blue negative, stronger colour = bigger number.

Nothing in score, soften or mix knows where a word is. Self-attention sees a bag of words: who bit whom is lost.

This is the price of dropping the RNN. An RNN knows order for free: it reads in order. Self-attention treats the input as a set: each word's score with "dog" is the same wherever "dog" sits, so the outputs are the same, just listed in a different order. The technical name is "permutation equivariant": shuffle the input, and the output is shuffled the same way, nothing more. The strips are real computations, done in the browser with random weights; the point holds for any weights.

The fix

Add a position to each word

Before the first layer, add a position vector to each word vector: xi + pi. Now “dog” at position 1 and “dog” at position 3 are different inputs.

The original Transformer used fixed waves: each column is a sine or cosine of the position, slower and slower to the right. Many later models (BERT, GPT) learn pi instead.

pi: one row per position (0–23), d = 32 columns

The formula, if asked: p(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 10000^(2i/d)), p(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 10000^(2i/d)). The left columns change fast, like the seconds hand of a clock; the right columns change slowly, like the hour hand. Together they give every position a unique pattern. The paper chose waves partly because a fixed offset (k positions later) is a simple rotation of the pattern, which may make relative positions easy to learn. Toggle the button: with positions, "dog" gets a different output in each sentence, so the model can tell who bit whom. Newer models often use other schemes that encode relative distance directly; that's beyond this course.

One head isn't enough

One head gives one blended answer

Everything so far, one WQ, WK, WV and one row of weights per word, is called one attention head.

In “the cat sat on the mat”, the word sat wants two things: who sat (cat) and where (mat).

The value of a word is its v = xWV, what it hands over when picked. Here vcat and vmat are random stand-ins, 8 numbers each. The 50/50 split is hand-picked: any split blurs, and 50/50 shows it most clearly.

One row of weights can spread over many words, but it hands back one average. Half “cat” plus half “mat” is a blur: a bit of both and all of neither.

A word often needs several kinds of context at once: its subject, its object, the word next to it, the noun a pronoun points to. One softmax mixes them all into one average, and averaging two different value vectors loses what each one said. Two separate heads, each free to look at one thing, keep "who" and "where" apart, and the next layer gets both. The vectors are random stand-ins for the value vectors of cat and mat.

Multi-head attention

Run several small attentions side by side

1
Split. Head h has its own small WQh, WKh, WVh: d → d/H numbers, so dk = dv = d/H.
Qh = XWQh
Kh = XWKh
Vh = XWVh
2
Attend. Each head does score, soften, mix on its own, in parallel.
headh = softmax( QhKh⊤ / √dk ) Vh
3
Join. Glue the heads end to end, and let one more learned matrix, WO (d × d), mix them.
Z = [ head1 ; … ; headH ] WO
original Transformerdheads Hdk = d / Hcost
base model512864about the same as one full-size head
The heads don't share weights, so each learns its own idea of what to look for. Because each head works with d/H numbers, eight heads of 64 cost about the same as one head of 512: you get eight different ways of looking for the same price. W_O is d × d; it lets the next layer use any combination of what the heads found. [a ; b] means glue end to end, as in Part 4. Nobody assigns jobs to heads: like Part 4's attention, they're learned from the loss alone. Also, d_k = 64 is the d_k from the √d_k slide.

Different heads, different jobs

head

Click a word to see where it looks. Weights are illustrative, in the style of what probing trained models finds.

Three patterns that people have found in trained Transformers: heads that look at the previous word, heads that link a word to the one it depends on grammatically (a verb to its subject), and heads that link a pronoun to the noun it refers to. A good paper on this is Clark et al. (2019), "What Does BERT Look At?". Try "sat" in each head, then "it". Caveat: real heads are messier than this, and many don't have a clean job.

Writing, not just reading

No peeking at the next word

A model that writes, one word at a time, learns by guessing each next word, all positions at once.

If “cat” could look ahead at “sat”, guessing “sat” would be trivial. So before softmax, every score for a later word is set to −∞: its weight becomes exactly 0.

This is masked self-attention: the decoder of the original Transformer, and the whole of GPT.

Weights in the map are illustrative.

This is teacher forcing from Part 3, done in parallel. The RNN decoder never saw the future because it hadn't computed it yet. A Transformer sees the whole target sentence at once during training, so the mask is what keeps it honest. When generating, it still writes one word at a time, feeding each word back in, as in Part 3. An encoder like BERT's has no mask: every word sees both sides, which is what "bidirectional" means in its title. Weights shown are illustrative.

Putting it together

One Transformer block

  • Self-attention moves information between words. The feed-forward network (two layers; 512 → 2048 → 512 in the original) then works on each word on its own.
  • The skip connections (x + sublayer(x)) are a short path for blame, like the LSTM's cell state in Part 2. With the norm layers (layer normalisation: rescale each word's numbers to a steady size), they're a big part of why stacks of 6 (original Transformer), 24 (BERT-large) or 96 (GPT-3) blocks train at all.
  • A translation Transformer also has Part 4's attention: the decoder's queries against the encoder's keys and values (cross-attention).
Part 6 opens this block up and builds the full encoder–decoder. The original stacks 6 of these blocks in the encoder. "Norm" is layer normalisation, which keeps each word's numbers at a steady scale; the original paper puts it after the add, and most newer models put it before the sublayer instead, which trains more stably. Ask: "which of the four earlier parts shows up here?" The skip path echoes Part 2's protected path; cross-attention is Part 4; the encoder–decoder shape is Part 3; and the idea of one set of weights used at every position is Part 1's parameter sharing.

The trade

RNN layer vs self-attention layer

RNNself-attention
steps that must wait for each otherT1
hops between two far-apart wordsup to T1
work per layerT · d²T² · d
knows word orderfor freeonly if you add positions
memory while generatingfixed: one hidden stategrows with everything written so far

T = words in the sentence, d = length of each vector. Rows 1–3 are Table 1 of “Attention Is All You Need”, and they count operations, not seconds.

Counting operations, self-attention is cheaper when the sentence is shorter than the vector size (T < d), which is most sentences. And in training it removes the RNN's step-by-step wait.

The first three rows are Table 1 of "Attention Is All You Need". They count operations, not seconds: real speed also depends on the hardware, the batch size and how well the code uses the GPU. With d = 512, a sentence of 50 words is far cheaper with self-attention; a book of 100,000 words is not, which is why long-context Transformers are an active research area. The last row is where RNNs still win: an RNN generating text carries one fixed-size state, while a Transformer has to keep keys and values for every earlier word. That's why RNN-like and "state space" models are being revived for long streams.

Be ready to answer

Questions

  1. What is self-attention? (every position scores every position of the same sequence, softmax, weighted sum of values)
  2. What are queries, keys and values? (what I'm looking for, what I offer for matching, what I hand over; three learned projections of each word)
  3. Why divide the scores by √dk? (dot products grow with dk; softmax saturates and gradients vanish)
  4. Why does a Transformer need position information? (self-attention is order-blind: shuffle the words, the outputs just shuffle)
  5. Why several heads? (one head returns one weighted average; several heads keep separate mixtures, each in its own learned space, at the same cost)
  6. What's the shape of the attention weights for T words? (T × T)
  7. Why mask the decoder? (so a word can't see the words it's being trained to predict)
  8. When does an RNN still make sense? (long streams with fixed memory, one step at a time)
Cover the hints, ask each question cold, and have someone answer out loud in one or two sentences. Question 1 should come with the equation: softmax(QKᵀ/√d_k)V.

Recap

Remember:

  1. Self-attention: every word makes a query, key and value; scores are q · k / √dk; softmax; mix the values.
  2. It's order-blind, so add a position to each word.
  3. Multi-head: several small attentions in parallel, glued and mixed. Stack blocks with skip connections: that's the Transformer.
