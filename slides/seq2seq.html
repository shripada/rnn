Recurrent Neural Networks · Part 3

Read first, then write

Sequence to sequence: one RNN reads, another one writes.

theblackcat →c→ lechatnoir
Parts 1 and 2 built one network that reads a sequence. Today we join two of them: an encoder that reads a whole sentence, and a decoder that writes a new one. The strip is the whole talk: three English words in, a single summary c in the middle, three French words out. Notice "black cat" comes out as "chat noir": the order flips.

The problem

Translation breaks the synced shape

Different length

I am hungry → J'ai faim

3 words in, 2 words out. A synced RNN has to give exactly one output per input.

Different order

the black cat → le chat noir

Output word 2, chat, depends on input word 3, cat. A synced RNN would have to write it before reading it.

So we read the whole input first, and only then start writing.

Two separate problems, one fix. Length: the output can be shorter or longer. Order: a word near the start of the output can depend on a word near the end of the input. In German the verb often goes to the very end of the sentence, so a synced model would have to guess it before seeing it. The only safe plan is to read everything, then write.

In every row, the output length isn't known until the model has read the input.

The pattern is general: any time both sides are sequences and they don't line up one to one. Speech is a good extreme case: audio frames vastly outnumber letters. Chatbots before 2017 were often seq2seq models trained on conversation pairs.

The idea

Two RNNs: an encoder and a decoder

Encoder: reads the input one word at a time. We keep only its final memory, the context c.

Decoder: starts from c and writes one word at a time, feeding each word back in, until it writes <eos>, a special “end of sentence” word.

This is the slide to draw on a whiteboard. Left: a many-to-one RNN, the encoder. Right: a one-to-many RNN, the decoder. The only wire between them is c. The two RNNs have separate weights: one has learned English, the other French. Lesson 2 calls this the encoder–decoder; papers also call it seq2seq.

A little history

Two papers in 2014

2014Kyunghyun Cho and colleagues (Montréal): the RNN Encoder–Decoder for translation. The GRU from Part 2 was invented in this same paper.
2014Ilya Sutskever, Oriol Vinyals and Quoc Le (Google): Sequence to Sequence Learning with Neural Networks. Two deep LSTMs, English → French.
2014–15Dzmitry Bahdanau, Cho and Yoshua Bengio add attention, which fixes the limits we'll meet at the end. That's next lesson's topic.
2016Google Translate switches to an LSTM encoder–decoder with attention.
Sutskever et al. describe it as one LSTM mapping the input "to a vector of a fixed dimensionality", and a second LSTM decoding the output from that vector. Good link back to Part 2: the GRU comes from Cho's encoder–decoder paper, so the two ideas were born together. The 2016 system is Google's Neural Machine Translation (GNMT, Wu et al.).

The encoder

The encoder is a many-to-one RNN

1
Read. Update the memory with each input word, exactly as in Part 1.
ht = f(ht−1, xt)  for t = 1 … T
2
Keep the last memory, the one after word T (the last word). That's the context. Every other output is thrown away.
c = hT
  • f can be a plain RNN, an LSTM or a GRU. In practice it's almost always gated: the first word has to survive until step T.
  • c has a fixed size, e.g. 256 numbers, however long the sentence is.
Nothing new here: it's the many-to-one shape, like sentiment classification, except that instead of turning the last memory into an answer we hand it over whole. Point out the fixed size: we'll come back to it. Sutskever et al. found it helped to feed the source sentence in reverse, so that the first source words end up close to the first target words.

The decoder

The decoder is our Shakespeare model, with a head start

1
Start from the context instead of from zeros.
s0 = c
2
Read the last word written. At step 1 that's a start token, <bos>.
st = g(st−1, yt−1)
3
Guess the next word. It gives a chance to every word it knows. We'll look at this list in a moment.
P(yt) = softmax(W st)

Stop when it writes <eos> (end of sentence). The decoder decides the length itself.

Compare with the demo's generate command: it's exactly a decoder, except its memory starts at zero (or from a priming string). Here the memory starts from c, so everything it writes is conditioned on the English sentence. g has its own weights, separate from the encoder's f. Cho et al.'s version also feeds c into every decoder step, not only the first; both work. The start and end tokens are ordinary vocabulary entries the model learns to use. Today's demo, demo/seq2seq.py, is exactly this: its decoder starts from the encoder's c and is fed ⟨s⟩.

Training

Encoder and decoder learn together, as one network

wrong guess at “noir”→ back through the decoder steps→ c→ back through the encoder steps
  • Training data: pairs of sentences, English and its French translation.
  • Loss: how surprised the decoder was by each correct French word, added up over the sentence. The same loss as our Shakespeare demo, once per output word.
  • Blame flows back through the decoder, through c, and into the encoder. The encoder learns to pack whatever the decoder needs into c.

Nobody tells the encoder what c should contain. It learns that from the decoder's mistakes.

Backpropagation through time, as in Part 1, just across a longer chain: the decoder steps, then the hand-off, then the encoder steps. That's why the encoder is almost always an LSTM or GRU: blame for the first output word has to travel back through the whole decoder and the whole encoder.

Training · a shortcut

If the decoder guesses wrong, what do we feed it next?

Recall: each decoder step takes the previous word as its input. Correct answer: le chat noir. At step 1 the model wrongly writes un.

During training
we know the right answer, so we feed it in

stepinputmodel writes
1<bos>un ✗
2lechat
3chatnoir

Step 2 gets “le”, not the wrong “un”. One slip doesn't ruin the rest.

When translating for real
no right answer exists, so it gets its own guess

stepinputmodel writes
1<bos>un ✗
2un?
3??

The mistake becomes the next input, so errors can pile up.

Feeding the correct previous word during training is called teacher forcing: like a teacher prompting the right word whenever a student stumbles while reciting.

Walk the left table first: "the model said 'un', but what do we hand it at step 2?" Let the class answer: we know the answer is 'le', so we give it 'le'. Then the right table: at translation time there's no answer key, so it must use its own 'un'. Benefit: training is faster and more stable, because every step learns from a correct context. Downside: the model never practises recovering from its own mistakes, yet that's exactly what happens in real use. Our Shakespeare demo did the same thing without naming it: each training input was the true next character, not the model's guess. Optional for stronger classes: this train/use mismatch is called "exposure bias", and one fix ("scheduled sampling") occasionally feeds the model's own guess during training. If asked whether it matters in our date demo: we measured it (train --no-teacher-forcing), and on that easy task it makes no consistent difference; the random seed mattered more. It earns its keep on long outputs with big vocabularies.

Writing · what the decoder outputs

The decoder doesn't output a word. It outputs a list of chances.

Same as our Shakespeare model. The last layer gives a score to every word it knows, and softmax turns those scores into chances that add up to 100%. Translating “a black cat”, step 1:

le50%
un40%
une6%
la3%
others1%

“others” = the remaining ~30,000 words in its vocabulary, each with a tiny chance.

So at every step we have to decide: which word from this list do we actually write?

Connect to Part 1: the Shakespeare model output a chance for each of 65 characters; here it's a chance for each of ~30,000 French words. Same softmax, bigger list. The numbers are illustrative. Ask the class: "Which word would you pick?" Most will say "le", the top one. That's the setup for the next slide: picking the top word at every step isn't always the best plan.

Writing · picking words

Don't lock in the first word too early

The obvious plan is to write the top word from the list each time. But look one step ahead, at the chances for the second word:

Greedy: always write the top word. It grabs le and is stuck with it.
le, then chat: 50% × 40% = 20%

Beam search: keep the best few options open (here, 2) and choose once you can see where each leads.
un, then chat: 40% × 90% = 36% ← wins

Analogy: a satnav. The road that starts out fastest isn't always the fastest route. Greedy commits to the best-looking first word; beam search keeps a handful of half-written sentences alive and picks the best whole sentence at the end. Why multiply? The chance of two things both happening is one chance times the other, like two coin flips both landing heads (50% × 50% = 25%). So a whole sentence's chance is the word chances multiplied along the path, and un→chat (36%) beats le→chat (20%) even though le looked better at step 1. Real systems use beams of about 4 to 10, and adjust for length, otherwise short sentences win just because they multiply fewer numbers. The demo slides later show a real case from our date model, where beam search fixes greedy's mistake.

Try it · the bottleneck

Squeeze the whole sentence into c

Like the narrow neck of a bottle, c limits how much gets through. It's the same size for 3 words or 45, so the longer the sentence, the less room each word gets.

The France sentence from Part 2. Drag to the end: the decoder has to write "French" from a 256-number summary of 45 words. Cho et al. (2014, "On the Properties of Neural Machine Translation") measured this: translation quality of the basic encoder–decoder drops sharply as sentences get longer. Gates help the memory survive, but they can't make c bigger.

Limitations

Where the basic encoder–decoder struggles

  1. One summary for any length. c holds the same 256 numbers for a 3-word sentence or a 45-word one. Long sentences lose detail, and translations of long sentences get noticeably worse.
  2. The decoder can't look back. Once it has c, it never sees the English sentence again. Writing the 20th French word, it can't check which English word it's meant to be translating.
  3. Early words fade. The first English word has to survive every encoder step before it reaches c. Gates help, but a lot has been written over it by then.

It's like translating a long speech from memory after hearing it once, with no notes.

More dates, same c

One date fits through c. More don't. Longer input, same c: the bottleneck, measured.

Numbers from demo/seq2seq.py: train --max-dates 4, then test --max-dates 4 (about 5 minutes to train). Ask before revealing: "it's perfect on one date; what happens with two?" It always gets the FIRST date right (99%), then the rest are mostly wrong: it writes the right number of dates, but the later ones are garbled, e.g. 1990-05-05 comes out as 1904-05-06. The two checks on the small line matter: training longer barely helps, but a bigger c does, so c really is a limit. Honest caveat if asked: even with 512 numbers it only gets two dates right 20% of the time, and 3–4 dates still fail. Part of the difficulty is the decoder learning to pull a second date back out of one summary, not only the size of c. With 3–4 dates and the big c, the last date does slightly better than the middle ones (9% vs 0%), a hint that recent input survives best. Full table (whole answer, 1/2/3/4 dates): c=128 99.3/0.6/0/0; c=128 trained 3× longer 100/3.7/0/0; c=512 100/20.2/0.1/0. This is the cue for attention: instead of one c, let the decoder look back at every encoder memory.

Recap

Remember:

  1. An encoder RNN reads the whole input into a context; a decoder RNN writes the output from it, one word at a time, until <eos>.
  2. Both are trained together: blame flows from the decoder's mistakes, through c, into the encoder.
  3. One fixed-size c is a bottleneck: long sentences lose detail, and the decoder can't look back at the input.
