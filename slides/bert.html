BERT = Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

The Transformer's encoder on its own, trained on billions of words by guessing hidden ones, then taught a new task in minutes.

the man went to the [MASK] to buy milk→ store 47%
Part 6 ended with three families: encoder–decoder, decoder-only and encoder-only. Today is the encoder-only one, BERT, from Google in 2018. Two things are new compared with every earlier part. First, we don't train it: we download a model someone trained for days on 3.3 billion words, and look inside. Second, the training task is unusual: hide some words, guess them from both sides. The 47% on the title is the real model's answer to this exact sentence. The name explains itself: Bidirectional (every word sees both sides), Encoder (Part 6's left half only), Representations (what it hands you is a vector per word, not text), from Transformers (built from Part 6's blocks).

Where we left off

Keep only the encoder. Then what?

Part 6's encoder reads a whole input and gives back one vector per token, each one built from every other token, left and right.

That's exactly what you'd want for understanding text: is this review positive? Which words are names? Where in this paragraph is the answer?

But in Part 6 the encoder only learned because a decoder above it was writing dates. On its own, what does it practise on?

Keep the Part 6 diagram in mind: BERT is the left column only, the encoder blocks, with no decoder and no cross-attention. Every earlier model in this course was trained on one task with labelled examples: next character, dates. Labelled examples are expensive. Plain text is nearly free. The question for today is how to train on plain text alone, and then reuse the result.

The big idea

Pretrain once, fine-tune many times

The expensive part, learning how language works, is done once, on text nobody had to label. Every task after that starts from it.

This is the shift BERT made standard in language: before it, most models were trained from scratch per task, with at most a pretrained word table (word2vec) at the bottom. After it, you start from a pretrained network and adjust all of it a little. Image models had already worked this way for years (pretrain on ImageNet, fine-tune on your photos). The analogy for an interview: pretraining is a general education, fine-tuning is job training. Pretraining costs days on specialised hardware; fine-tuning on a laptop takes minutes, as our demo will show.

The story in one breath: word2vec gave words a fixed vector, learned from plain text, but one vector for every meaning. ELMo, from early 2018, used exactly the LSTMs from Part 2 to give each word a vector that depends on its sentence; it read left to right and right to left with two separate networks and glued the results. GPT, mid-2018, used a Transformer and pretrain-then-fine-tune, but only left to right. BERT, October 2018, combined the Transformer with reading both sides at once, and set new records on eleven language benchmarks. The word2vec row connects back to the word demo's neighbours command.

Three small changes: the positions are a learned table (512 rows) rather than sine waves; the feed-forward uses GELU, a smooth version of ReLU; and there's a third embedding added at the bottom, the segment, which we'll see next. Try it · the real tokenizer

Words become WordPiece tokens

  • A fixed list of 30,522 pieces: common words whole, rare ones in parts (## = joins the piece before). No word is ever unknown.
  • [CLS] starts every input; its top vector will stand for the whole input. [SEP] ends each sentence.
  • Each token's input vector = token embedding + segment (A or B) embedding + position embedding.
This is BERT's real tokenizer, running in the browser on its real 30,522-piece vocabulary (uncased: everything is lowercased and accents removed first). Under each token: its id in the table, and its position. Try a long rare word, your own name, or a word with an accent. WordPiece is from Google's translation system (Wu and colleagues, 2016): pieces are chosen so that common words stay whole and anything can still be spelled. Characters were our unit in Parts 3 to 6, words in the word demo; pieces sit between: a small table, no unknown words, short sequences. Type a sentence B and you'll see the segment change: B tokens get the second segment embedding, that's how BERT tells two sentences apart.

The problem

Why not just “predict the next word”?

GPT can train on plain text by predicting the next word, because its mask hides the future. BERT has no mask: every word sees every word. Asked to predict the next word, it would just look at it, and in a 12-block stack, information about every word reaches every position.

The fix: don't hide the future. Hide the word itself, and ask for it back.

This is the key reasoning step and a common interview question: "why can't BERT be trained as a normal language model?" Because a normal language model needs each position to not see its answer, and the whole point of BERT is that nothing is hidden by direction. ELMo got around this by running two separate one-directional LSTMs. BERT's answer: corrupt the input. Replace some words with a [MASK] token, so the answer isn't in the input at all, and predict them from everything else.

Pretraining task 1

The masked language model

Pick 15% of the tokens. Make the model guess each one from the vector at its position, through a layer that scores all 30,522 pieces. Loss only at the picked positions.

Of the picked tokens:

80% → [MASK]80%
10% → a random word10%
10% → left alone10%
one training input

Green: replaced by [MASK]. Rust: replaced by a random word. Underlined: picked, but left as it was. The model must give back the original at all three kinds.

Why not mask all of the picked 15%? Because [MASK] never appears when BERT is later used on real text. If the model only ever had to work hard at [MASK] positions, its vectors for ordinary words might be lazy. The random 10% means any token might be wrong, so the model has to check every word against its context; the unchanged 10% means the right word sometimes is the input, so it doesn't learn to always distrust it. Why only 15%: mask too much and there's too little context left; too little and each pass gives few training signals. That's one cost of BERT versus GPT: GPT learns from every position, BERT from about 15% of them. Click "pick again" to see a fresh random choice; the proportions are the real recipe, on our example sentence.

Pretraining task 2

Next sentence prediction

Input: [CLS] A [SEP] B [SEP]. Half the time B really followed A in the book; half the time it's a random sentence.

The [CLS] vector at the top goes through one small layer: IsNext or NotNext. That's where [CLS] learns to sum up the input.

Meant for tasks about pairs: does this answer fit the question? Does sentence B follow from A?

P(IsNext) from the real model, every A with every B. Green: the real follow-on.

It's easy: a random B is usually about something else, so topic alone gives it away. Swap the milk pair's order and it still says IsNext, 100%. RoBERTa (2019) dropped this task and did better.

The real numbers are nearly all 0% or 100%, which tells you how easy the task is. Our demo: `uv run demo/bert.py nsp "the man went to the store ." "he bought a gallon of milk ."` gives IsNext 100%, and it prints the reversed order too, also 100%. So the model learned "same topic", not "comes next". RoBERTa (Yinhan Liu and colleagues, Facebook, 2019) trained BERT's architecture longer, on more data, without next sentence prediction, and beat it. ALBERT replaced it with a harder version: are these two sentences in the right order? For an interview: know both tasks, and know that the second one turned out not to matter much.

Numbers from section 3 and appendix A of the BERT paper. Sequences were mostly 128 tokens long, and 512 for the last 10% of rounds, to learn the far positions. 1,000,000 × 256 × up to 512 tokens is roughly 130 billion token positions; about 40 passes over 3.3 billion words. Wikipedia was used for its text only, no lists or tables. The cost is why almost nobody pretrains their own BERT: you download it, as our demo does.

What do the heads look at?

Rows: blocks 1–12. Columns: heads 1–12. Click one.

The real attention weights of all 12 × 12 heads for two sentences that differ in one word: "too tired" (it = the animal) and "too wide" (it = the street). Part 5 used this example with made-up weights; these are measured. Block 7, head 11 is the one I found that switches: for "tired", "it" looks at "animal" (28%) more than at "street" (7%); for "wide", it looks at "street" (59%). Other heads, like block 4 head 11, look at "animal" both times, whichever is meant. Most of the grid is something else: many heads in the middle blocks put most of their weight on [SEP], a kind of "nothing to do here" default (Kevin Clark and colleagues, "What Does BERT Look At?", 2019). Don't oversell: attention weights show where a head looked, not why the model decided.

Fine-tuning

One small layer on top, for each task

The new layer is tiny (768 × 2 weights for a yes/no task). Then train all of it, BERT included, gently: learning rate 2–5 × 10−5, 2–4 passes over your data.

This is Figure 4 of the BERT paper, the four task shapes. Whole-input answers read the [CLS] vector. Per-token answers read every token's vector through the same small layer: names, places, parts of speech. Question answering, as in SQuAD: the question is segment A, the paragraph is segment B, and two small vectors score every paragraph token as the answer's start or end. Sentence pairs: both sentences in, [CLS] out. Compare with Part 1's task shapes: many-to-one is [CLS], synced many-to-many is per-token tagging. What's NOT here: writing new text, like translation or chat. That needs a decoder.

SST-2: short movie-review phrases, positive or negative. We train on a random 2,000 of its 67,349 and grade on the 872 test sentences.

Numbers from one run each (seed 0) on a laptop CPU: the fine-tune took about 3 minutes, 250 rounds of 16 sentences. "No pretraining" averages learned word vectors, trained from random on the same 2,000 sentences: it has never seen most words in the test set. Frozen BERT uses the downloaded vectors as they are and learns only the 1,538 weights of the new layer. Fine-tuned BERT adjusts all 110 million. The paper's number used all 67,349 sentences and a GPU. The grading set is SST-2's dev set, as is usual, because the official test labels are hidden.

The contrast

BERT vs GPT

Same blocks. The difference is the mask and what it's asked to guess.

The one-line interview answer: BERT is a bidirectional encoder trained by masked language modelling, used for understanding tasks with a small head on top; GPT is a left-to-right decoder trained to predict the next word, used to generate text. Since about 2020, large GPT-style models have taken over most tasks, because a big enough writer can also be asked to classify. But BERT-style encoders are still widely used where speed matters and no writing is needed, for example turning documents into vectors for search. What came after BERT, by name only: RoBERTa (same model, better training), DistilBERT (smaller, faster), ELECTRA (spot the replaced word instead of guessing it).

Questions

  1. What does BERT stand for, and who made it? (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers; Devlin, Chang, Lee and Toutanova, Google, 2018)
  2. What is BERT, architecturally? (a stack of Transformer encoder blocks: no decoder, no mask; base = 12 blocks, d 768, 12 heads, 110 M weights)
  3. How is it pretrained? (masked language model: 15% of tokens picked, 80/10/10 [MASK]/random/same; plus next sentence prediction)
  4. Why can't it be trained to predict the next word? (with no mask, each position can see the next word: the task would be trivial)
  5. Why the 80/10/10 split? ([MASK] never appears in real use; random and unchanged tokens make every position's vector useful)
  6. What are contextual embeddings? (a word's vector depends on its sentence: “bank” by a river ≠ “bank” with money; word2vec gives one vector)
  7. How do you fine-tune it for sentiment? Name entities? ([CLS] vector → small layer; each token's vector → small layer; train everything, small learning rate)
  8. BERT vs GPT in one sentence. (both-sides encoder for understanding vs left-to-right decoder for writing)

Recap

Remember:

  1. BERT = Part 6's encoder, big, with no mask: every token reads both sides.
  2. Pretrained on plain text by guessing hidden words, because with both sides visible, “next word” would be cheating.
  3. It gives each word a vector in context; fine-tuning adds one small layer and adjusts everything a little.
Ask someone to explain, without slides, why a bidirectional model needs masking and a left-to-right one doesn't.

The whole course

From a hidden state to BERT

An RNN carries a memory forward (1). Gates keep it alive (2). An encoder and a decoder translate (3). Attention looks back (4), then replaces the RNN (5). The whole Transformer (6). Its encoder, pretrained on everything (7).

Questions?

That's the arc of the course, and a good way to answer the classic interview question "how did we get from RNNs to Transformers?": the RNN's single hidden state was a bottleneck for long-range information and for speed; attention fixed the first, dropping recurrence fixed the second, and pretraining on huge amounts of text made the result useful everywhere.
