Vanishing Gradients

How RNNs are trained, and why they forget anything far back.

By the end you can explain backpropagation through time, show why the gradient shrinks or blows up as it travels back through the steps, and name the standard fix for each problem. “Why do vanilla RNNs struggle with long sequences?” is probably the most-asked RNN interview question, and this lesson is the answer.

Warm-up: Lessons 1 & 2, mixed

Answer from memory, then reveal.

In the Lesson 1 simulator, what happened to the spike's memory when |wh| was below 1?
It faded a little more at each step, because the signal kept getting multiplied by a number smaller than 1. Hold on to that: this lesson is the same effect running backwards.
Sentiment classification is which shape, and which hidden state feeds the classifier?
Many-to-one. The classifier uses the final hidden state hT, because it's the only one that has seen the whole sentence.
What does a seq2seq encoder hand to the decoder?
A fixed-size context vector c, which is the encoder's final hidden state.

Backprop in two sentences

You know backprop conceptually. Here's all of it that this lesson needs:

  1. A gradient is a sensitivity: if I nudge this number slightly, how much does the loss move? Training nudges each weight in the direction that lowers the loss.
  2. The chain rule says that sensitivities multiply along a path. If nudging A moves B three times as much, and nudging B moves C twice as much, then nudging A moves C 3 × 2 = 6 times as much.

That's it. Keep “multiply along the path” in mind; the rest of the lesson follows from it.

Backpropagation through time (BPTT)

To train an RNN you unroll it (Lesson 1) and run ordinary backprop over the unrolled chain. D2L: This procedure requires us to expand (or unroll) the computational graph of an RNN one time step at a time.1 Because the chain is laid out in time, it's called backpropagation through time.

A twist: one weight, many contributions

Lesson 1's parameter sharing has a consequence here. Whh is used at every step, so its gradient is the sum of a contribution from every step: how much the loss would change through step 1's use of Whh, plus step 2's, and so on.1 The contributions from early steps are the ones that would teach the network long-range patterns. The next section shows why those are the ones that get lost.

Why the gradient vanishes or explodes

Take the one-number RNN from Lesson 1: ht = tanh(wh·ht−1 + wx·xt). How much does a nudge to ht−1 move ht?

∂ht / ∂ht−1 = wh × tanh′(…)   =: f, the per-step factor

tanh′ is the slope of tanh. It's at most 1, and it gets close to 0 when tanh saturates near ±1.

By the chain rule, sensitivities multiply along the path. Going back k steps multiplies k of these factors together:

signal reaching k steps back ≈ f × f × … × f = fk
Per-step factor fAfter 10 stepsAfter 50 stepsResult
0.5≈ 0.001≈ 10−15Vanishes: early steps get almost no learning signal
0.9≈ 0.35≈ 0.005Still vanishes, just more slowly
1.011Stable, but only exactly at this knife edge
1.5≈ 58≈ 6 × 108Explodes: huge updates, unstable training

In a real RNN the factor is a matrix (Whh multiplied by the tanh slopes), and what decides the outcome is how much that matrix stretches or shrinks a vector. D2L puts it in terms of eigenvalues: eigenvalues smaller than 1 vanish and eigenvalues larger than 1 diverge.1 This was analysed by Bengio et al. in 1994 and in detail by Pascanu, Mikolov & Bengio.2

Same effect, both directions. In Lesson 1, the memory of the spike faded going forward. Here the learning signal fades going backward. Both happen because the same factor is applied over and over. Colah's version: as the gap grows, RNNs become unable to learn to connect the information.3

Play: watch the gradient travel back

The loss sits at the last step. Each bar shows how much gradient reaches that step, on a log scale where each gridline is 1000× the one below it. Bars that fall off the bottom are effectively zero.

○Vanish. With T ≥ 20, get the signal reaching step 1 below 0.001.
○Explode. With clipping off, push the signal reaching step 1 above 1000.
○Tame it. Keep it exploding (above 1000 unclipped), then turn on clipping so no bar exceeds θ.
○Knife edge. With T = 50 and clipping off, keep the signal reaching step 1 between 0.5 and 2. Notice how small the window is.

Two things to notice from that:

  • Healthy long-range gradients need f ≈ 1 exactly. Training doesn't hold the weights at a knife edge, and tanh′ is usually below 1 anyway. So in practice vanishing is the default.
  • Clipping shrank the exploding bars but didn't lift the vanishing ones. All clipping can do is scale the gradient down.

The fixes, and which problem each one solves

ProblemStandard fixHow it works
ExplodingGradient (norm) clippingIf the gradient's overall size exceeds a threshold θ, scale it down to θ, keeping its direction. Proposed by Pascanu et al.;2 D2L calls it vital for preventing the occasional massive gradients from destabilizing training.4
VanishingChange the architecture: LSTM / GRUAdd a path through time that the signal can travel without being squashed at every step. That's the next lesson. Clipping does not address the problem of vanishing gradients.4
Cost of long sequencesTruncated BPTTOnly backpropagate a fixed number of steps (τ) instead of the whole sequence. Cheaper and more stable, but it deliberately gives up on very long-range gradients.1

“RNNs are trained with backpropagation through time: unroll the network and backprop through the chain. The gradient reaching a step k positions back is a product of k per-step Jacobians, roughly Whh times the tanh slope. If that factor is below 1 the gradient shrinks exponentially, so the network can't learn long-range dependencies. If it's above 1 the gradient explodes. Exploding is handled with gradient clipping. Vanishing needs an architectural fix, like the LSTM's gated cell state.”

Check yourself

1. In BPTT, why is the gradient for Whh a sum over time steps?
Parameter sharing (Lesson 1). One Whh is used at every step, so each step contributes a piece of its gradient.
2. The per-step factor is 0.5. Roughly how much signal is left 10 steps back?
0.510 = 1/1024 ≈ 0.001. The factors multiply; they don't add. That's why the decay is exponential.
3. Which problem does gradient clipping solve?
Clipping can only scale gradients down. A vanished gradient has nothing to scale back up, which is what you saw in the Tame it challenge.4
4. Why does the tanh activation make vanishing more likely?
tanh′ ≤ 1, and it's near 0 when saturated, so every step's factor gets multiplied by something at most 1. (Its output ranges over −1…1, so it isn't always positive.)
5. A vanilla RNN reads a 100-word review whose sentiment hinges on word 3. What goes wrong?
The loss is at step 100 (many-to-one), and its signal has to travel back about 97 steps to reach word 3. After that many multiplications it's essentially zero, so the network can't learn that word 3 mattered. The words do share weights, so “the weights for word 3 differ” is wrong.
6. Truncated BPTT trades away what, in exchange for what?
Stopping the backward pass after τ steps makes training cheaper and more stable, but the gradient no longer includes contributions from further back.1

Say it out loud

Explain vanishing gradients in an RNN in 30 seconds.
Training unrolls the RNN and backprops through time. The gradient reaching k steps back is a product of k per-step factors (Whh × tanh slope). If those are below 1, the product shrinks exponentially, so early inputs get almost no learning signal and the network can't learn long-range dependencies.
Exploding vs vanishing: which fix goes with which, and why can't clipping fix both?
Exploding → gradient clipping (rescale the gradient down to a threshold). Vanishing → architectural change (LSTM/GRU). Clipping can only shrink a gradient; it can't recover a signal that has already decayed to almost zero.
Synced many-to-many has a loss at every step. How does that change which steps get gradient?
Every step now gets a strong gradient from its own nearby loss. But the long-range part (how step 3's loss depends on step 1) still has to travel back through the chain and still vanishes. So the model learns local patterns well and long-range ones poorly.
